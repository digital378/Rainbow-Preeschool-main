Diwali Activities for Kindergarten

A festival-of-lights guide for little ones aged 1.5 to 5.5 — 2026

Diwali activities for kindergarten can be simple, bright and full of meaning. This preschool guide gathers stories, crafts and safety ideas for parents and teachers.

1Chapter

Let’s Celebrate!

A little glow, a little colour and time together: the simplest ingredients can make a festival feel special to a child.

What is Diwali?

Diwali, also called Deepavali, is often known as the festival of lights. Families brighten homes, arrange diyas, create rangoli and share festive food.

Diwali stories remind us that good can overcome what is unkind or frightening, and hope can return after a difficult time. Different families and regions celebrate in their own ways. A child can notice a lamp, place paper flowers, listen to a story or enjoy a sweet with loved ones.

Explain it simply: “Diwali is a time when many families celebrate light, kindness and being together.” Keep the conversation open. Children may ask why a diya has a flame, what a rangoli means or why a relative celebrates differently.

The five days of Diwali

Diwali is a five-day festival. Customs differ from home to home; here are names children may hear, with no dates needed to enjoy the stories.

Day one

Dhanteras

Families prepare for the festival; children can help tidy or make a decoration.

Day two

Naraka Chaturdashi

Known by different names in different places, it is part of the joyful lead-up.

Day three

Diwali · Lakshmi Puja

Families light lamps, share prayers or quiet moments, and enjoy being together.

Day four

Padwa / Govardhan Puja

Many communities mark this day with gratitude, family customs and special food.

Day five

Bhai Dooj / Bhau Beej

Siblings and loved ones celebrate affection and the care they share.

A little guide by age

For toddlers, Diwali can mean noticing warm lights, bright colours and familiar faces. Offer a large paper diya to decorate with chunky crayons, let them place big paper petals with you, or sing a short rhyme. Stay beside them around every lamp and choose battery lights where little hands can reach. There is no need for a long explanation; a few minutes of shared attention is plenty.

Preschoolers enjoy choosing colours, following a few craft steps and helping with a small family task. Tell them Diwali is a festival of lights and kindness, then invite them to sort paper shapes for rangoli or count pretend diyas. Keep scissors, small decorations and any real flame with an adult. Let your child lead the conversation and celebrate the process more than a neat result.

Kindergarteners can compare traditions and retell a short story in their own words. They can plan a repeating rangoli pattern, count lamps, dictate a greeting card or explain a craft to a friend. Make room for different customs: “Some families do this, and some do that” is respectful and true. Give children safe responsibility, such as arranging paper decorations or choosing a storybook.

2Chapter

The Stories

Read slowly, pause for questions and let pictures do some of the talking. These gentle retellings are starting points, not the only way a family may tell a tradition.

Ram, Sita and the lamps of Ayodhya

Long ago, Prince Ram, Sita and Ram’s brother Lakshman had been away from home. At last, they returned to Ayodhya. The people were happy to welcome them back. At evening, families placed little lamps outside so the path looked bright.

The lamps shone like tiny stars. Friends greeted one another, and the city felt joyful. Children can imagine waiting for someone they love and welcoming them home.

Many people remember Diwali through this homecoming story and the triumph of good over bad. Tell the version meaningful to your family. Ask, “How would you help someone feel welcome?”

Goddess Lakshmi and a cared-for home

Many families honour Goddess Lakshmi as a symbol of good fortune and wellbeing. Before Diwali, people may clean and brighten their homes, decorate a doorway or arrange flowers. Children can see this as making a welcoming place for loved ones.

Invite your child to return books, wipe a low table or arrange paper flowers. A clean home is useful every day; the festival gives families a reason to prepare together. The important part is not a perfect room, but caring for one another.

Some households pray to Lakshmi, while others observe different customs. Ask, “What makes our home feel welcoming?”

The little diya that found its glow

In a little clay shop sat a small diya named Tara. She watched the bigger lamps being chosen and wondered, “My bowl is tiny. Can my light matter?”

On Diwali evening, a family carried Tara home and placed her on a high, safe shelf beside an adult. Her wick glowed gently. Across the room, another lamp shone, and then another. Soon the whole window looked warm and welcoming.

Tara saw a child smile at the bright room. “I may be small,” she thought, “but I can be part of something lovely.” The family thanked one another for helping prepare. Tara learned that a little kindness, like a little light, can help everyone feel welcome.

A gentle thought
Small acts of care matter, especially when we share them.

3Chapter

Diwali in Maharashtra and Across India

One festival holds many local customs, foods, stories and family memories. Share what is familiar, and make space for other traditions too.

A Maharashtra celebration

In Maharashtra, children may see colourful rangoli near a doorway and an akash kandil—a decorative lantern—hanging outside a home. Families prepare faral, a collection of festive snacks. Chakli, karanji, laddoo and shankarpali are familiar examples, though recipes and favourites vary. Children can help name shapes, set out napkins or watch an adult prepare food.

Some children build a small mud killa, or fort, with family or friends. This miniature play tradition can spark talk about hills, walls and imagination. A child can use blocks or modelling dough and invent a story about who lives in the fort. Choose age-appropriate materials and supervise closely.

Padwa and Bhau Beej are also part of the season for many families. Customs may include visiting relatives, sharing food or expressing affection. Ask grandparents and caregivers what they remember from childhood; the answers can become treasured family stories.

Different regions, different traditions

Bengal

Kali Puja

Many families worship Goddess Kali around this festive time, with lights and regional customs.

South India

Deepavali morning

Some homes include an early morning oil bath and festive clothes, alongside other customs.

North India

Govardhan Puja

Many families mark this day with offerings and stories connected with Lord Krishna.

Customs vary; invite children to notice both shared and local traditions.

4Chapter

Crafts, Rangoli & Activities

These Diwali activities for kindergarten use everyday materials and leave room for children’s choices. An adult should prepare cutting jobs and stay close throughout.

Ages 2–5

Paper plate diya

Make a bright paper lamp with no flame.

  1. Cut a plate into a shallow bowl shape.
  2. Glue large paper flame shapes above it.
  3. Add dots and a kind wish.
Ages 3–6 · supervised

Clay or play-dough diya

Shape a lamp-like bowl with soft dough.

  1. Roll child-safe dough into a ball.
  2. Press a hollow and pinch a rim.
  3. Decorate with large dough shapes; no real wick.
Ages 2–5

Handprint diya art

Turn a handprint into a keepsake picture.

  1. Make a print with washable child-safe paint.
  2. When dry, add a paper bowl and flame.
  3. Write the child’s name together.
Ages 3–6 · supervised

Easy dot-grid rangoli

Explore patterns with chalk, rice or petals.

  1. Draw a few large dots on paper.
  2. Join dots with chalk or arrange petals.
  3. Repeat colours; keep loose materials from mouths.
Ages 4–6 · adult cuts

Paper akash kandil

Create a lantern decoration, never a light source.

  1. Prepare a folded strip and pre-cut fringe.
  2. Decorate the paper with large shapes.
  3. Roll, glue and hang away from heat.
Ages 3–6

Diwali greeting card

Send a message to someone the child loves.

  1. Fold a sheet of thick paper in half.
  2. Decorate with a diya or rangoli drawing.
  3. Let the child dictate a greeting inside.
Ages 3–6 · adult cuts

Paper plate Lakshmi / lotus craft

Make a lotus-inspired paper decoration.

  1. Cut large petals from coloured paper.
  2. Arrange petals around a paper centre.
  3. Glue and add a kind wish.
Ages 2–6 · supervised

Firework paint art

Make quiet, colourful bursts on paper.

  1. Pour washable paint into a shallow tray.
  2. Stamp a clean fork outward from a dot.
  3. Use puffy paint only when child-safe and labelled.
Ages 3–6

Counting diyas

Make early maths hands-on with pretend lamps.

  1. Draw large diya shapes on card.
  2. Place a number card beside each group.
  3. Count aloud, touching one picture per number.
Ages 2–5

Rangoli colour-matching game

Notice colour, shape and position through play.

  1. Make large circles or petals in a few colours.
  2. Lay out a simple rangoli pattern card.
  3. Match a shape or continue the pattern.
Ages 3–6 · supervised

Mini killa fort

Build an imaginative miniature fort.

  1. Choose chunky blocks or non-toxic modelling clay.
  2. Build a hill, wall or pretend gateway.
  3. Invent a story; keep small parts from toddlers.

Diwali activities for the kindergarten classroom

Circle-time story

Read a gentle Diwali story, pause at pictures and invite children to share what “welcome home” feels like.

Rangoli group art

Offer a large paper outline and pre-cut shapes. Each child adds a petal, dot or colour to a shared design.

Diya-counting station

Use paper diya counters and number cards to count, sort small groups and compare which row has more.

Sort pretend sweets

Use large paper shapes or play food, not real sweets. Group by shape or colour, then describe the pattern.

Kandil-making corner

Set out pre-cut paper panels, glue sticks and broad crayons. Let children design with an adult nearby.

“Light” vocabulary words

Explore glow, bright, lamp, pattern and welcome. Draw a word, act it out or use it in a sentence.

Safe & Happy Diwali

Children can enjoy colour and togetherness while adults take responsibility for flames, noise and small materials. Adjust ideas to each child and setting.

A parent’s and teacher’s safety guide

  • Keep young children well away from firecrackers, sparklers and open flames. Do not let a child light, hold or pick up fireworks; choose a calm activity instead.

  • Use battery-operated LED diyas near children. If an adult lights a traditional lamp, keep it stable, away from curtains and curious hands, and never leave it unattended.

  • Loud sounds can be upsetting. Offer a quiet room, familiar comfort item or suitable ear protection, and allow a child to leave noisy places without pressure.

  • Supervise craft time. Choose age-appropriate, non-toxic supplies; adults handle scissors and hot glue, and small parts stay away from children who may mouth them.

  • Keep familiar meals, rest and bedtime steady. Offer water, breaks and a calm place during a busy celebration.

  • Choose a greener celebration: use petals or chalk for rangoli, reuse paper decorations and reduce smoke and noise.

  • Consider pets, neighbours and older people. Give pets a secure quiet space with water, and choose considerate hours and volume.

A small plan for a calmer celebration

Before the day, explain what to expect and agree on a quiet place to take a break. Keep water and a familiar snack nearby; children may step away whenever they need.

Adults set the pace. A child can enjoy Diwali with a simple card, family snack or kind greeting.
