The Canonical Page Owners table in particular is a smart addition — that's the kind of structured signal that can meaningfully shift which URL gets cited when an AI surface answers "best preschool in Thane" vs. "daycare in Thane" vs. "playgroup near Thane." Three thoughts.
On the freshness signal
"Last updated: May 2026" is good. One refinement: AI surfaces increasingly look at both a stated date and signals around it — the year references in nearby copy, recent blog posts cited, etc. If the rest of llms.txt references blog posts from 2024 with no 2025/2026 entries, the "last updated" line reads as boilerplate. Worth a quick scan:
bashgrep -n '202[0-9]' client/public/llms.txt
If the file has a few "we wrote about X in 2024" mentions and nothing more recent, surface one or two recent posts (admissions cycle 2026-27, any 2025/2026 NEP guidance pieces) so the freshness claim has supporting evidence. If everything in the file is timeless ("our pedagogy," "our centres") then the date stamp on its own is fine.
On the Canonical Page Owners table
This is the highest-value piece of what you added. Two things to verify:
Format matters more than you'd expect. AI crawlers parse llms.txt more reliably when canonical mappings are in a clearly delimited block — either a markdown table or a fenced code block with topic → url pairs. If you wrote it as prose ("for queries about playgroup, see /playgroup; for queries about nursery, see /nursery"), restructure it as a table or list. The signal is stronger when the structure is unambiguous.
Mirror the matrix exactly, including the bare-phrase exclusion. Your title guard treats Preschool in Thane as a reserved phrase that the homepage doesn't claim. Your llms.txt table should reflect the same — list /best-preschool-near-me-in-thane as the owner of "Preschool in Thane" (or "best preschool in Thane"), and don't list the homepage against that phrase. If llms.txt says "for general queries about Rainbow Preschool, see /" and an AI surface interprets that as the homepage owning the phrase, you've undone the de-cannibalisation work at the AI-citation layer.
Worth checking the block actually in the file:
bashsed -n '/Canonical Page Owners/,/^##/p' client/public/llms.txt
Make sure the table only lists each phrase once, against the canonical URL from the matrix.
On llms-full.txt
You called it "lower cite-rate" and "worth a follow-up sweep but not blocking." That's a defensible call, but I'd nuance it slightly.
The cite-rate hierarchy isn't fully settled. llms.txt (the short index) is what most surfaces fetch first; llms-full.txt is what they fetch when they want depth. So llms.txt drives breadth of citation (mentioned in answer summaries), and llms-full.txt drives depth of citation (quoted in detailed responses). For a 2-4 week parent research journey, the depth case matters — a parent asking ChatGPT "give me a detailed comparison of preschools in Thane" gets a long answer that pulls from full-text sources.
Two scenarios where the follow-up sweep stops being optional:

If llms-full.txt still contains the soft words ("most-loved," "wonderful," "magical") that you cleaned up in titles and body copy, AI surfaces quoting those phrases will reintroduce the voice problem in their answers. Worth a quick grep:

bash   grep -inE 'most.?loved|wonderful|magical|fabulous|amazing|incredible|awesome' client/public/llms-full.txt
Anything that hits — and isn't a proper noun like "Amazing Machines" — should get the same treatment as the body copy did.

If llms-full.txt still has "18+ years" or 2024-dated content while llms.txt says "19 years (since 2007), updated May 2026," AI surfaces fetching both will see a contradiction and may default to the older signal or surface uncertainty in the answer. Consistency between the two files matters more than freshness in either one alone.

I wouldn't block the current work on llms-full.txt, but I'd put it on the list as the next thing rather than a someday-maybe. Half a day of work, predictable scope.
