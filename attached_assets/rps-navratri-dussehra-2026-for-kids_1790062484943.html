What is Navratri? What is Dussehra?

Navratri means “nine nights.” It is a happy festival where we celebrate a kind and powerful Goddess for nine days in a row. Each day, we remember a different, gentle form of her — sometimes brave, sometimes soft and loving, sometimes wise. Families dress up, light little lamps called diyas, dance, and spend time together.

After the nine days comes one big, joyful day called Dussehra (also called Vijayadashami). On this day, we remember a story about being brave and kind, and about good winning over bad. In the evening, many families go outside to watch a giant paper figure being burned in a bonfire, with lots of twinkling fireworks in the sky. Everyone claps, and children love watching the lights.

So in the simplest words: Navratri is nine days of happy Goddess celebrations, and Dussehra is the big, sparkly bonfire day right after — a joyful reminder that being good and brave always wins in the end.

A Little Guide by Age

For your littlest one, Navratri and Dussehra are simply about colours, twinkly lights, and happy music. They don’t need to understand the stories yet — just let them enjoy! Dress them in a bright colour, let them see the diyas glow (from a safe distance), clap along to some fun dandiya beats, and cuddle up close during family time. If they see you happy and excited, they will feel happy and excited too — that’s really all Navratri needs to mean for a toddler.

Preschoolers are just starting to enjoy a good story! Gently tell them that Navratri celebrates a kind, powerful Mother Goddess who takes care of everyone, and that each day has its own special colour to wear. They’ll enjoy picking their outfit for the “colour of the day” and might like a simple little dance. Keep it short: “a brave, loving Goddess protects everyone” is enough for now. Introduce Dussehra as “the big fireworks day” — simple crafts like colouring a diya or making a paper crown are perfect for this age.

Kindergarteners are ready for a little more of the “why.” They can understand nine special days, each honouring a different form of the Goddess, each with its own colour. Share a gentle version of the Durga story — how she used her light and courage to keep everyone safe. They’ll enjoy hearing about Ram, Sita and Hanuman, and why Dussehra is celebrated with a big bonfire called Ravana Dahan. Be ready for sweet “who” and “why” questions — simple, warm answers work best.

The Stories

Gentle retellings, just right for little ears — about a Goddess who protects with light, and a prince who never gave up.

The Story of Maa Durga

Long, long ago, there was a very powerful and loving Mother Goddess named Durga Maa. She was kind to everyone, and she loved to take care of the whole world, just like your mother takes care of you.

One day, there was a big, scary monster who was making everyone feel frightened and sad. The monster was so strong that nobody else could stop him. But Durga Maa was not afraid — she knew that kindness and courage together are more powerful than anything scary.

Durga Maa gathered all her light, her love, and her bravery. She rode on a big, gentle lion, and she carried many special gifts in her many hands — each one a symbol of a different kind of strength, like patience, hope, and courage. She was calm, strong, and full of light, like the brightest, warmest sunshine.

When the scary monster came close, Durga Maa used her light and her courage to stop him, gently but firmly, and to keep everyone safe. Once he was gone, the whole world could breathe easy again. Everyone came out of their homes, smiling and thankful, and celebrated with lamps, flowers, music, and dancing.

That is why, every year, for nine special nights, we remember Durga Maa. We light little diyas, wear bright colours, and dance because we are thankful she keeps everyone safe — a reminder that when we are brave, kind, and full of light inside, we help make the world feel safer for everyone around us.

The Story of Ram & Ravana

Once upon a time, there was a prince named Ram, known far and wide for being gentle, honest, and very brave. He had a loving wife named Sita, and a dear, loyal friend named Hanuman, who was strong, cheerful, and always ready to help.

One day, something sad happened — Sita was taken far away by a powerful king named Ravana, who was not kind. Ram loved Sita very much, so he set off on a big journey to bring her home safely, with Hanuman and many friends joining him along the way.

Ram and his friends travelled a long way, faced many challenges, and never once gave up. Ram always stayed calm, kind, and brave, even when things felt difficult. Finally, with his courage and the help of his friends, Ram was able to bring Sita safely back home. Ravana’s unkind ways could not stand in front of Ram’s goodness and bravery.

Everyone was overjoyed! To celebrate this happy ending, people built a big statue of Ravana out of paper and lit it up in a bonfire, with bright, sparkling fireworks lighting up the sky. This joyful celebration is called Dussehra, or Ravana Dahan.

Every year, we remember this story and celebrate this same happy evening. Children love watching the twinkling fireworks and the big, glowing bonfire from a safe distance with their families — a reminder that being kind and brave always finds its way to a happy ending.

The most twirly, colourful part of Navratri — and it’s just as fun for wobbly toddler dancers as it is for confident kindergarten performers.

Garba & Dandiya Fun

One of the happiest parts of Navratri is Garba and Dandiya — a fun, circular dance where everyone moves together, clapping or gently tapping small decorated sticks called dandiya in rhythm with cheerful music. You don’t need to be an expert dancer to join in! Even toddlers can happily sway, clap, and giggle in the middle of the circle, while preschoolers and kindergarteners love learning the simple twirl-and-clap steps.

Try a mini Garba session at home — some cheerful music, a couple of dandiya sticks (even wooden spoons work in a pinch!), and a few giggly spins is all it takes for a joyful evening together.

Did you know friends in other parts of India celebrate a little differently too? In Bengal, families visit beautifully decorated pandals for Durga Puja. In parts of South India, homes display little dolls stacked on steps, called Golu. And in North India, families enjoy watching Ramlila, a colourful play that tells the story of Ram. One big festival season, so many beautiful ways to celebrate it!

Kid-Friendly Stories

The Little Diya Who Was Afraid of the Dark

Once, in a small clay shop, there lived a tiny diya named Chintu. Chintu was scared of the dark. “What if my light is too small?” he worried.

On Navratri night, Amma placed Chintu on the windowsill and filled him with oil, then lit his little wick. Chintu was surprised — his flame was small, but it glowed so warm and bright! Outside, the whole street was dark, but one by one, other diyas lit up too, until the whole lane twinkled like stars had come down to visit.

Chintu smiled. “My little light matters,” he said happily. “Together, we made the dark go away!” From that day, Chintu was never afraid again.

Moral
Even a small light can help chase away the dark, especially with friends beside you.

Mia Helps Decorate for Garba

Mia loved swirly skirts and jingly bells. This Navratri, her building was decorating the courtyard for Garba night. “Can I help?” asked Mia. Aunty smiled and gave her a basket of marigold flowers to hang along the gate.

Her friend Zoya was tying colourful flags, and little Arjun was placing diyas in a neat circle. Everyone worked together, sharing ribbons and giggles. When evening came, the courtyard sparkled with flowers, flags, and twinkling lights, and children twirled in circles, clapping and laughing.

Mia looked around proudly. “We made it beautiful together,” she said. Mummy hugged her tight. “Sharing made it even more fun, didn’t it?”

Moral
When friends share and help each other, celebrations become extra joyful.

Fun Craft & Activity Ideas

Ages 2–4

Paper Plate Diya

Cut a paper plate in half and stick torn yellow/orange/red paper to look like a glowing flame — a simple, mess-friendly way to talk about light and warmth.

Ages 3–5

Dandiya Stick Decorating

Wrap colourful ribbons around two sticks and add stickers, then tap them together gently to the beat of festive music.

Ages 4–5

Friendly Recycled Ravana

Turn a small cardboard box into a smiley, friendly Ravana face with paper and crayons — big eyes and a funny moustache, all giggles, no scares.

Ages 1.5–5

Colour-of-the-Day Dress Up

Pick a colour each morning and help your little one find something to wear in that shade — joyful, and no shopping needed.

Ages 2–5

Mini Garba Twirl Time

Play cheerful Garba music and hold hands in a circle, taking turns to twirl slowly and clap — a lovely way to move and giggle together.

Ages 3–5

Durga Crown Craft

Decorate a paper crown strip with sequins and colours, then wear it proudly as a little festive queen or king for the day.
