Let's Make It Magical!

Ganesh Chaturthi is the ten-day festival when we welcome home a special friend — Ganpati Bappa, the elephant-headed friend who loves modaks, listens with his big ears, and helps us feel brave when things feel hard. For our tiniest learners, this isn't a lesson in history or mythology — it's a season of colours, music, sweet treats, and cuddly stories that plant the very first seeds of culture, curiosity, and joy.

At Rainbow Preschools, we believe little ones learn best through wonder, not worksheets. So this guide is full of gentle stories, simple rhymes, easy craft ideas, and tiny speeches — everything a parent needs to make Ganesh Chaturthi 2026 warm, joyful, and just the right amount of exciting for a toddler or preschooler.

What Is Ganesh Chaturthi? (In Words a Little One Can Understand)

Ganesh Chaturthi is a happy festival when families welcome a clay friend named Ganpati Bappa into their homes for a few days. Bappa has a cute elephant face, big round ears, and a soft round tummy, and everyone sings, dances, and eats sweet modaks to celebrate him. After some days together, families say a loving goodbye and take Bappa to the water, waving and singing "Ganpati Bappa Morya!" as he goes home — with a promise that he'll come back again next year.

This year, Bappa comes to stay from Monday, 14th September to Friday, 25th September, 2026 — ten whole days of songs, sweets, and celebration! Here's a simple family timeline:

India Turns Its Streets Into a Festival — Ganeshotsav in Maharashtra & Thane

Here in Thane, and across Maharashtra, Ganesh Chaturthi isn't just celebrated at home — it fills the whole city with joy! Streets are decorated with twinkling lights and flowers, drummers called dhol-tasha players fill the air with big, happy beats, and families walk together to see beautifully decorated Bappas set up by their neighbourhoods, called "mandals." For a little one, it's a wonderful sensory adventure: bright colours to look at, joyful music to dance to, and the happy chant of "Ganpati Bappa Morya!" echoing everywhere you go.

The Story of How Ganpati Bappa Was Born (A Story for Little Ones)

Read this aloud together — perfect for bedtime or story circle time.

Once upon a time, there lived a kind and powerful goddess named Parvati. One day, before taking a bath, she wanted someone to guard her door. So, with loving hands, she made a little boy out of soft clay and breathed life into him. "Stand here," she said gently, "and don't let anyone in until I'm done." The little boy nodded proudly, happy to help his mother.

Soon, a stranger arrived at the door and tried to walk straight in. The little boy stood tall and said, "You cannot enter — my mother is bathing!" But the stranger, who was actually Lord Shiva and did not know this was Parvati's son, grew upset that a little boy would stop him from entering his own home.

When Parvati came out and saw what had happened, she explained that the boy was her own beloved son. Shiva felt very sorry, and wanting to make things right, he asked his helpers to bring the head of the very first creature they saw. They returned with a baby elephant's head, and gently, lovingly, it was placed on the little boy — and he opened his eyes, alive again, with the sweetest elephant face anyone had ever seen.

Everyone loved him so much that all the gods came to bless him. They gave him a new name — Ganesha — and said that from that day on, he would always be remembered first, before starting anything new, because he was so wise, so kind, and so very loved. And that, little one, is how our beloved Ganpati Bappa came to be!

More Bedtime Stories for Little Ones

Ganesha and His Tiny Best Friend, the Mouse

Ganpati Bappa may be big, but his very best friend is tiny — a little mouse named Mushak! One day, Mushak was scurrying about so fast that he bumped into things and made a big mess. Ganesha smiled and said, "Slow down, my friend. Even the smallest of us can do the biggest things — but only with a calm heart." From that day on, wherever Ganesha travels, gentle little Mushak carries him with pride, showing us that no one is ever too small to be someone's best helper.

Why Ganesha Loves Modaks So Much

Once, Ganesha's mother Parvati made a big plate of sweet modaks and told her two sons, Ganesha and his brother Kartikeya, "Whoever is the most thoughtful may have the sweetest one." Kartikeya rushed off to do a big, brave task to win the prize. But Ganesha simply sat beside his mother, gave her a warm hug, and said, "Amma, you are the sweetest thing in my life." Parvati laughed with joy and gave him the sweetest modak of all — because kindness, she said, is always the tastiest thing.

Ganesha and the Moon Who Learned to Say Sorry

One night, after enjoying a few too many modaks, Ganesha took a little tumble. Up in the sky, the Moon laughed and laughed at him. Ganesha, a little sad, gently said, "It's alright to laugh, but it's not alright to be unkind." The Moon felt sorry and asked for forgiveness, and Ganesha, being ever so kind, forgave him at once. That's why, even today, we're told to be gentle with our words — just like Bappa always is.

Growing With Bappa: A Little Guide by Age

Every age group enjoys Ganesh Chaturthi a little differently — here's a gentle guide for parents:

  • Playgroup (around 1.5–2.5 years): Simple sensory joys — touching soft flowers, listening to gentle aarti music, watching twinkling lights, and tasting a tiny nibble of modak (checking for any allergies first).
  • Nursery (around 2.5–3.5 years): Short stories, clapping along to rhymes, pointing out Bappa's big ears and round tummy, and simple pretend play with a soft toy elephant.
  • Junior KG (around 3.5–4.5 years): Enjoying the full birth story, trying simple craft activities, learning the "Ganpati Bappa Morya" chant by heart, and helping decorate the puja thali.
  • Senior KG (around 4.5–5.5 years): Reciting a short speech, attempting simple essays or show-and-tell lines, understanding the idea of an eco-friendly Bappa, and enjoying the fun quiz with a parent.

A Simple, Safe Modak Recipe to Try at Home

Always supervise young children fully around cooking, hot surfaces, and check for any food allergies first.

You'll need: rice flour, water, grated coconut, jaggery, a pinch of cardamom powder.

Steps for grown-ups (with little helpers watching or assisting safely):

  1. Gently cook grated coconut with jaggery and cardamom until soft and fragrant — this is your sweet filling.
  2. Knead rice flour with hot water (adult step only) into a soft dough.
  3. Let your little one help flatten small dough balls into little rounds with clean hands.
  4. An adult fills each round with the coconut-jaggery mixture and shapes it into a modak.
  5. Steam for about 10 minutes, cool completely, and enjoy together!

Fun Craft & Sensory Activities for Preschoolers

1. Clay Modak Making (Sensory Play)

What you need: soft playdough or clay, a small bowl.

How to do it: Help your little one roll a small ball of dough and gently pinch one end into a little peak, just like a modak! No need for perfection — the joy is in the squishing and rolling.

2. Elephant Ear Headband Craft

What you need: a paper strip, grey/orange paper, glue, crayons.

How to do it: Cut two big round "ears" from paper, decorate them together, and glue them onto a headband strip. Watch your little one giggle as they become Bappa for the day!

3. Flower Thali Decoration

What you need: a plate, flower petals (real or paper), colours.

How to do it: Let your child arrange petals freely on a plate to make a pretty welcome thali for Bappa — a lovely lesson in colours, patterns, and gentle hands.

4. Footprint Elephant Art

What you need: washable paint, paper.

How to do it: Paint your little one's foot and gently press it onto paper — with the heel as the elephant's head and toes as the trunk! Add ears and eyes once dry for an adorable keepsake.

5. Eco-Friendly Home Ganesha (with an Adult's Help)

What you need: natural shadu clay (available at most craft or puja stores).

How to do it: Together, shape a small, simple Bappa from natural clay. It's a beautiful, hands-on way to introduce little ones to caring for our environment while celebrating.

6. Colour-the-Bappa Sheet

What you need: a simple printed outline of Ganesha, crayons or washable paints.

How to do it: Let your child colour freely, however they like — there are no wrong colours for Bappa! A lovely quiet-time activity during the festival week.

7. Sound Box: Bappa's Favourite Sounds

What you need: a small box or basket, a tiny bell, dried leaves, a scrap of soft cloth.

How to do it: Fill the box with a few gentle, safe items that make different sounds (a bell for the aarti, crinkly leaves for flowers). Let your little one explore each sound with their hands — a wonderful way to link the festival to sensory discovery.

8. Match-the-Modak Game

What you need: paper cut-outs of a few simple shapes (a mouse, a flower, a modak, an ear).

How to do it: Ask your little one to find and match pairs, naming each item aloud as "Bappa's mouse," "Bappa's modak," and so on — a simple, playful way to build vocabulary around the festival.

Ganesha's Special Symbols — Explained for Kids

  • The Mouse (Mushak): Bappa's tiny best friend and ride — showing us that even small friends can be the mightiest helpers.
  • The Modak: Bappa's favourite sweet treat, shaped like a little dumpling.
  • Big Ears: So Bappa can listen carefully to everyone who needs him.
  • The Flower (Durva grass & hibiscus): Soft green grass and pretty red flowers that we offer to Bappa with love.
  • The Elephant Face: What makes Bappa so easy to spot and so lovable to everyone!

A Tiny Festival Glossary for Parents

New to some of these words? Here's a quick, simple guide you can also share with your little one:

  • Sthapana: The day Bappa's idol is welcomed and installed at home or at a mandal.
  • Aarti: The joyful evening prayer song, sung with a lit lamp, welcoming and thanking Bappa.
  • Modak: Bappa's favourite sweet dumpling, made from rice flour or wheat, filled with coconut and jaggery.
  • Visarjan: The gentle goodbye ceremony, when Bappa's idol is immersed in water at the end of the festival.
  • Mandal: A community group that sets up a large, beautifully decorated public Ganesha for the neighbourhood to visit.
  • Anant Chaturdashi: The tenth and final day of the festival, when the big farewell (visarjan) takes place.
  • Shadu clay: Soft, natural clay used to make eco-friendly idols that dissolve safely in water.

Why Do Some Families Celebrate for 1.5 Days and Others for 10?

You may notice some homes welcome Bappa for just one and a half days, while others — like many mandals — keep him for the full ten. This is simply a lovely family or community tradition passed down over generations, often based on convenience, space, or long-held customs, and there's no single "right" number. What matters most, as you can gently tell your little one, is the love and joy shared during whichever days Bappa is home.

Parent's Guide: Celebrating Ganesh Chaturthi at Home With Toddlers

  • Fancy dress ideas: A simple orange or yellow kurta, a little crown, or DIY elephant ears (see craft activity above) make an adorable, comfortable "mini Bappa" costume — no need for anything elaborate or heavy.
  • An eco-friendly first Ganesha: Choosing a small natural shadu clay idol is a gentle, hands-on way to introduce sustainability, and it's safe to immerse in a bucket of water at home.
  • Involve little hands in the puja: Let your toddler help place flowers, ring a small bell, or clap along to the aarti — participation matters far more than perfection.
  • Keep it short and joyful: Toddlers engage best in short bursts — a 5-minute aarti, a story, and a sweet treat is plenty for a memorable celebration.
  • Talk about saying goodbye gently: Visarjan can be an emotional moment for little ones. Frame it warmly: "Bappa is going home to rest, and he promises to come back and visit us again next year!"

Keeping Little Ones Safe & Happy During the Festival

  • Crowds and processions: If you're taking your toddler to see a mandal or a visarjan procession, keep them close, choose less crowded viewing times if possible, and be ready to step back if the noise or crowd feels overwhelming for them.
  • Loud sounds: Dhol-tasha beats are joyful but loud — carrying soft ear protection for very young or sound-sensitive children is a thoughtful precaution.
  • Sweets and allergies: Modaks typically contain coconut, jaggery, and sometimes nuts or ghee — always check ingredients before offering a taste to a child with known allergies.
  • Sun and heat: September afternoons in Thane can still be warm; a hat, water bottle, and shaded viewing spot go a long way during outdoor celebrations.
  • Water safety near immersion sites: Keep a firm hold of little hands near ponds, tanks, or the sea during visarjan — the excitement of the crowd can make it easy for a small child to wander.
